InWorld

From imagination to living worlds with agents and fast video generation

Murea Labs Technical report, 2026

Read the paper arXiv, coming soon Cite

Illustration based on an InWorld-generated frame.

Overview

InWorld is an interactive world you can see and hear. You say what happens next. A world agent decides how the story moves, and a fast audiovisual generator renders it as video with sound. Each new segment is checked against the plan and written back into the world, so later turns build on what actually appeared on screen.

The agent, Murea-Agent, keeps track of characters, objects, places, and narrative progress. The generator, Murea, comes in two configurations: Murea-Pro, the flagship model for multi-turn interaction, and Murea-Fast, a lightweight few-step model built on LTX-2.5. Branching histories let you return to an earlier decision and try another path, and co-op mode puts several players' actions into the same shot.

Read the abstract

Interactive world models aim to simulate environments that evolve under user actions while retaining the consequences of past interactions. Extending such worlds to sight and sound requires persistent entities, changing scene states, and local audiovisual dynamics to remain coherent across turns. This paper presents InWorld, an interactive audiovisual world model that couples explicit world-state management with neural audiovisual generation. Its world agent, Murea-Agent, maintains characters, objects, locations, and narrative progress, interprets user actions in context, and compiles planned events into generation conditions. Observations of generated frames inform subsequent planning and selected state updates, closing the interaction loop. Its audiovisual generator, Murea, realizes these events through Omni-Reference Generation, using visual references to anchor entity appearance and structured descriptions to specify actions and dialogue. Streaming generation uses modality-specific histories, with longer audio context for unfinished utterances. Degraded-history training prepares the model for imperfect rollout context, while sequential MeanFlow and distribution matching distillation enable few-step synthesis. Branching histories support alternative continuations, and co-op interaction brings multiple participants' actions into the same world.

We evaluate the generation components in complementary configurations. Murea-Pro achieves an Overall score of 81.13 on WBench Full-289, the highest among the compared systems. Murea-Fast achieves a Total score of 64.98 on OpenS2V-Eval and an aggregate score of 69.9 on OS2V-Pro, an extended evaluation of reference fidelity, attribute binding, rendering quality, and audio.

A world agent with explicit memory

The generator keeps no memory between calls, and there is no simulator behind the world. So Murea-Agent keeps an explicit, inspectable world state outside the generator, plans in natural language, and checks every rendered segment against what it intended.

Report section 2

\(\mathcal{W}_t\) World state \(\mathcal{E}\;\mathcal{S}\;\mathcal{N}\;\mathcal{G}\;\mathcal{T}\)

Step 1 of 4

Plan the next event

The planner, a multimodal language model, reads a snapshot of the world state, your instruction, a duration budget for one segment, and the final frame of the previous segment. The symbolic state says who exists and what they look like; the frame shows how the last segment actually ended.

It returns a directive: the location, the participating assets, the event, dialogue and speakers, how the new segment connects to its parent, and proposed state changes. A request that needs more than one segment is split into ordered events that fit the budget, and only the first group is rendered now.

\[\sum_{\beta\in B_j}\hat{d}(\beta)\le\bar{d},\qquad j=1,\dots,J\]
Example from the report

“Go to the market and talk to the guard.” This turn shows the walk to the market. The conversation with the guard is rewritten as a complete instruction and becomes the input to the next turn. You can interrupt at any point.

Step 2 of 4

Compile the conditions

A deterministic compiler turns the directive into generator conditions: prompt text, ordered reference images, and voice references. Selected characters go first, then other characters and focal objects, then the location. Voices follow who is speaking, never a reference's position, so a place or an object cannot be handed a voice by accident.

Prompts use the six-section format the generator was trained on: subject definitions, a summary, reference-retention statements, the shot, the soundscape, and music. The compiler also decides whether to continue from the parent segment's latents or cut to a new shot.

\[c_t=\big(y_t,\ \mathcal{R}_{v,t},\ \mathcal{R}_{a,t}\big),\qquad \mathcal{R}_{v,t}=\big(I_{e_{(1)}},\dots,I_{e_{(n_t)}}\big)\]
In the example

The market is new, so it is registered with a generated reference image before it first appears, and its soundscape is fixed on this first visit for reuse later. Walking into it is a continuous move, so the segment continues from the previous one. A jump to a distant known place would start a new shot from that place's reference image.

Step 3 of 4

Render video and sound together

Murea jointly generates the segment's video and audio. Reference images anchor each entity's appearance, optional voice samples supply timbre, and on continuation the parent's tail latents carry pose, motion, camera, and layout across the boundary.

Planning happens before rendering, so its latency adds directly to waiting time. The planner emits the fields needed for rendering first, and directives for the suggested next actions are prepared in the background, so choosing a suggestion skips a planning call.

\[X_t\sim p_\theta\big(X\mid c_t,\ \tilde{Z}_t\big),\qquad \tilde{Z}_t=\begin{cases}Z_{h_{t-1}} & \text{continue}\\ \varnothing & \text{cut}\end{cases}\]
In the example

The parent's latents keep the character's pose and the camera moving smoothly out of the previous segment, while the registered reference image keeps the character recognisable on the way to the market.

Step 4 of 4

Observe what actually happened

Whether a requested change happened can only be seen in the frames. While the segment plays, an observation model compares its final frame with the planned event and the current characters and inventory, then updates next-turn suggestions, the objective, and which items were picked up or dropped.

Observation runs asynchronously, so playback never waits for it. A result is applied only if the world is still at the version that was observed. If you have already continued, undone a turn, or edited the world, the stale result is discarded.

\[\hat{o}_t=\rho\big(F_t,\,D_t,\,\mathcal{E}_t\big),\qquad \mathcal{W}\leftarrow\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{obs}}\big(\mathcal{W},\,\hat{o}_t\big)\ \text{ only if }\ \mathrm{ver}(\mathcal{W})=\mathrm{ver}(\mathcal{W}_t)\]
In the example

The final frame is checked against the planned walk. Items the character visibly picked up, like the backpack in the overview figure below, are added to the inventory, and the conversation with the guard waits as the next turn.

\[D_t=\pi\big(u_t,\,\Sigma(\mathcal{W}_{t-1}),\,F_{t-1}\big),\quad c_t=\mathcal{C}\big(D_t,\,\mathcal{W}_{t-1}\big),\quad X_t\sim p_\theta\big(X\mid c_t,\,\tilde{Z}_t\big),\quad \mathcal{W}_t=\mathcal{U}\big(\mathcal{W}_{t-1},\,D_t,\,X_t\big)\]

One turn: instruction \(u_t\), state snapshot \(\Sigma\), previous final frame \(F_{t-1}\), directive \(D_t\), generator condition \(c_t\), optional parent latents \(\tilde{Z}_t\), rendered segment \(X_t\).

What the world remembers

Persistent symbolic state is the long-range memory; audiovisual latents are the short-range context.

\(\mathcal{E}\)
Asset registryEvery character, object, and location, each with a reference image, an optional voice sample, and a locked description that is reused word for word. Returning to a known place retrieves the same image instead of describing it again.
\(\mathcal{S}\)
Dynamic stateWhat changes: a character's location, outfit, visible state, mood, goals, and carried items; a location's time of day, weather, lighting, and props.
\(\mathcal{N}\)
Narrative contextThe main storyline and its milestones, side quests, the current objective, and a turn history of instructions and one-line summaries. Generation prompts are left out so planning stays about the story.
\(\mathcal{G}\)
Place graphConnections between locations, so movement follows known paths and the agent can tell a continuous walk from a jump.
\(\mathcal{T}\)
Segment treeEvery generated segment as a node with its directive, prompt, reference order, frames, continuation link, and state snapshots from before and after.

Branches and shared play

  • Undo, regenerate, branchUndo restores the state saved before a segment and moves back to its parent; the next turn grows a sibling. Regenerating also adds a sibling. Branches never overwrite each other, and registered assets and place connections are shared by all of them.
  • Action contracts for each playerIn co-op, each input becomes a structured contract of actor, action, target, path, and end state. Contracts are prepared as soon as a player submits, while the others are still deciding.
  • One joint shotA director model plans composition, camera, staging, and timing, and a writer drafts the shot with one placeholder per action. The compiler then inserts each locked contract exactly once, bound to its actor.
  • Forecasts are not factsEach round forecasts a world event and suggests an action for each player. Only what the observation model sees in the keyframes enters the confirmed history.
  • Less waitingWhile a segment plays, the next joint shot is rendered speculatively for the suggested actions. It is kept only if the submitted round matches it exactly: same head node, shared assets, characters, and action text.

One generator for sight and sound

Murea turns the agent's conditions into synchronized video and audio. Text specifies the scene, actions, and dialogue; reference images specify appearance; optional voice samples specify timbre. The three parts below address reference fidelity, sampling cost, and temporal continuity.

Report section 3

Omni-Reference Generation

Each reference image is encoded by the video VAE into one clean latent frame. Each voice sample becomes an audio-VAE latent sequence that keeps its timing, rather than a single speaker embedding. References join the target along the token dimension, up to 20 per modality, and are never noised or supervised.

Learned slot IDs tell references apart. In rotary position coordinates, images sit at \(-N_v,\dots,-1\) s before the target and voice samples end at \(-0.04\) s, so they read as context, not as past events. Audio–video cross-attention excludes reference tokens in both directions; reference information reaches the other modality only through the target.

MeanFlow, then distribution matching

Stage one, audiovisual MeanFlow, learns the average velocity over a time interval under a frozen teacher, so one model can sample in a single step or several. Its Jacobian–vector product runs through a custom FlashAttention-4 kernel.

\[x_r^m=x_t^m+\Delta_m\,u_\theta^m(x_t,t,r;c)\]

Stage two, Distribution Matching Distillation, starts from the MeanFlow weights. The score difference between the frozen teacher and an auxiliary denoiser that tracks the student guides it toward better visual detail and motion. The two stages run one after the other.

Streaming with bounded memory

For long rollouts the model extends the stream one interval at a time. Video keeps one preceding chunk. Audio keeps several, so an unfinished sentence and its prosody carry across the boundary instead of restarting or repeating words.

In training, the history comes from ground truth and is deliberately degraded with noise or latent perturbations, preparing the model for the imperfect context it meets at inference. The same MeanFlow-to-DMD recipe extends to streaming with the history added to the conditions.

From minutes to seconds

Long audiovisual token sequences make every denoising step expensive. Distillation cuts the number of steps; the rest of the stack cuts the cost of each step. Together they take one 5.04-second clip from 231.6 s to 6.96 s on eight NVIDIA H20 GPUs.

Results

The two generator configurations are evaluated on complementary benchmarks: Murea-Pro on multi-turn interaction, Murea-Fast on reference-conditioned generation. The full agent loop is specified by the system design rather than scored end to end.

Report section 5

Multi-turn interaction on WBench

WBench Full-289 has 289 cases and 1,058 interaction turns covering navigation, subject action, event editing, and perspective switching. Each turn receives the instruction and the final frame of the model's own previous segment, so later turns are judged in the context the model itself produced.

Murea-Pro has the highest Overall score, 1.71 points above Kling 3.0. The lead comes from Interaction (+9.52) and Physical (+5.23). It is within 0.05 of Kling 3.0 on Quality and trails it on Setting (−4.48) and Consistency (−1.70).

Per-metric scores against four baselines

Murea-Pro leads all four on navigation trajectory, event-edit, subject-action, and perspective-switch adherence, and causal fidelity. By task it scores 90.92 on event editing, 87.04 on subject action, 80.65 on perspective switching, and 71.79 on navigation, its weakest task.

30100Bold and underlined: best in the row

Reference-conditioned generation

OpenS2V-Eval's Open-Domain split has 180 cases across seven reference configurations, from a single face to several people with objects. OS2V-Pro is our extended evaluation of the same clips, adding per-reference fidelity, attribute binding, rendering quality, and audio. Murea-Fast uses eight distilled steps and generates 121 frames at 24 fps.

On OpenS2V-Eval, Murea-Fast scores 64.98, the highest among open-source models, with the best NexusScore of any system. Seedance 2.0 scores 67.13 but returned 148 of 180 clips; on those 148 shared cases the gap is 1.02 points. On OS2V-Pro, Murea-Fast scores 69.9, 0.2 below Kling V3 Omni, with the best per-reference fidelity and the joint-best identity consistency.

Murea-Fast is prompted with expanded captions, and OS2V-Pro's task-following (VTF) and binding (Bind) checklists are derived from them, which gives it an input advantage on those two metrics and on the OS2V-Pro aggregate. API systems were queried with the official prompts. Clips a system did not return are not scored as zero, so read results alongside clip counts. Audio quality (ATQ) and event–sound alignment (VESA) are excluded from the aggregate; on both, Murea-Fast trails Kling V3 Omni (59.7 vs 66.3 and 75.0 vs 88.7).

Data and captions

Multi-reference audiovisual training needs captions that say which reference defines each entity, which voice belongs to each speaker, and when things are said and done.

The dataset combines data created for this work with a curated subset of HuMoSet. Automated filters screen resolution, duration, audio availability, visual quality with DOVER and LAION-Aesthetics, and reference–target identity with ArcFace across sampled frames. Human annotators then judge reference consistency, audio consistency, and video quality.

TransNet finds shot boundaries and speech recognition extracts transcripts with timing. Gemini 3.1 Pro then writes structured caption JSON from the pre- and post-cut frames, the transcript, and the visual and voice references. Each caption binds characters to their voice references, attributes every line of dialogue, and describes each shot with its start time.
